Cartagena’s Teatro Adolfo Mejía (TAM) officially opens its 2026 cultural season in early January with the 20th edition of the Cartagena International Music Festival. According to riotimesonline.com, this reinforces the venue’s role as the most important cultural stage in Colombia’s Caribbean region and a draw for culturally minded investors and expats.

The festival runs from 4 to 12 January, featuring 27 concerts across the historic walled city. TAM serves as the main night-time stage for symphonic and chamber music performances. The 2026 theme is ‘El alma y el cuerpo: universalismo musical y escuelas nacionales’ (The Soul and the Body: Musical Universalism and National Schools), exploring how composers balance a shared musical language with distinct national voices.

The Franz Liszt Chamber Orchestra returns as the resident ensemble, anchoring a programme that brings together soloists and conductors from Europe, Latin America, and beyond. Ticket prices and exact schedules are published on the festival’s official channels closer to the opening date.

TAM sits inside Cartagena’s walled city and is described by the Mayor’s Office as ‘one of the most important cultural settings in the country.’ The Instituto de Patrimonio y Cultura de Cartagena (IPCC) manages the theatre’s year-round programming from January through April, a window that anchors the city’s high season for cultural tourism. For property owners and hospitality investors, the January festival surge provides a reliable spike in footfall and premium room rates inside the walled city.

Traditional gaita music, played on long indigenous flutes and accompanied by drums, is deeply woven into Cartagena’s identity. The region hosts dedicated gaita festivals, including the Festival Nacional Autóctono de Gaitas in San Jacinto and the Festival de Gaitas, Cantos y Tambores in the El Socorro neighbourhood. At previous editions of the Cartagena International Music Festival, groups such as Gaiteros de San Jacinto have appeared in workshops and crossover concerts, creating a dialogue between classical forms and traditional Caribbean sounds. While the formal 2026 season opening at TAM is a classical music event, gaitas remain a living presence in Cartagena’s streets, plazas, and community festivals throughout the year.

After January, TAM’s season continues with the Hay Festival Cartagena, a major literary and ideas gathering, in late January or early February. The Cartagena International Film Festival (FICCI), the oldest film festival in Latin America, follows in March, using TAM for premieres and gala screenings. Jazz nights, choral performances, and theatre productions fill the gaps, making the theatre a year-round engine for the city’s creative economy.

For expats and long-stay visitors, this dual musical identity is part of the city’s appeal: a morning of symphonic rehearsal inside a restored 19th-century theatre can be followed by an evening of gaita and bullerengue in a neighbourhood square. The density of high-quality cultural events from January through April reduces the sense of seasonal isolation that can affect smaller Caribbean cities. The presence of a resident orchestra and a reliable festival calendar also creates demand for short-term rentals, hospitality services, and bilingual professional staff.

Colombia’s broader push to grow cultural tourism as a share of GDP, supported by ProColombia and local mayoral offices, means venues like TAM are likely to see continued public funding and international partnership. The 2026 season opening is a concrete data point in that longer trend.
